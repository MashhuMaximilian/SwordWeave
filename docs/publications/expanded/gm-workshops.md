## Prepare a first session people can enter

A first session should let new players make useful decisions before they understand every family of primitive. Prepare a visible problem, a few people with clear motives, and several plausible approaches. Teach the procedure as those approaches arise. You do not need to perform every rule in one evening.

:::art bridge

### Before anyone makes a character

Tell the group what kind of situation you are offering. “A canal settlement needs help before a storm” gives players more useful direction than a long list of permitted ancestries. Discuss tone, interpersonal expectations, subjects to avoid, and an easy way to pause or revise a scene. Nobody needs to justify a personal boundary in public.

Agree the starting progression budget, equipment allowance, and whether example constructions are accepted. A 25-BU start is useful for learning because the ledger is small. It is not compulsory. Do not promise that every possible capability at an example price will be valid in this campaign.

Ask for a purpose, a connection, and one ordinary competence from each character. A reason to cooperate can be small: shared employment, a borrowed boat, or an interest in the same missing person. They need enough to enter the scene together, not a completed biography.

Check their records with them. Ask each player to explain a capability, its ordinary use, one limitation, and the bonus they will add to a check. If someone cannot answer, help rewrite the capability before adding more options. A readable narrow build is a stronger teaching tool than a large unexplained package.

### Prepare facts instead of a sequence

Write what exists, what people want, and what is changing. Avoid “first they persuade, then they fight, then they repair.” A player may repair immediately, persuade someone unexpected, or leave to get help. Your prepared facts should survive those choices.

For the sluice example, the gate is physically jammed, a downstream bed needs protection, workers know a passage, and the rising water threatens routes. Those facts support several solutions. If the party creates a new safe path, use it. If they persuade the Watchers, the creatures do not need to attack anyway to satisfy your outline.

Give important information through more than one source. A worker knows the passage; a painted maintenance arrow points toward it; an old map shows it. You can still hide optional secrets. The immediate problem should not stop because one die failed to reveal the only usable route.

### Teach the first roll without shorthand

Describe the uncertainty before naming a practice. Show the full calculation: die, current attribute, applicable training, other modifiers, and DC. Then show the combined sheet bonus and explain that it already includes those contributions.

Use a small consequence the group can understand. A failed diagnosis might cost time and change a route. If injury or death is a real stake, establish it clearly. Do not use a first-session failure to teach that the DM can attach an unrelated punishment after the roll.

When advantage appears, explain conditions and cancel opposing stacks. One remaining stack means two dice; two means three. Select the die and add the static bonus once. A visible example is easier to remember than a paragraph of probability theory.

### Introduce a round when timing matters

Use Council, Fast, Measured, and Heavy when actions meaningfully depend on or oppose each other. Explain that the round is shared. Gather coherent Main Intents and ask which need another result. Give the opposition visible behavior.

At first, keep the number of independent enemy workings small. A Watcher turns a current; a second guards a spawning bed. Their different intentions create a useful scene without requiring a hidden action list. Complexity determines track placement; output size is a different question.

Track movement and one ordinary independent Reaction Slot. A player moving 10 ft in Fast with Speed 30 has 20 ft left, not a new allowance in Measured. A reaction needs an actual trigger, opportunity, and scope. Explain these when someone proposes a response, rather than interrupting the opening scene with every exception.

### End with a playable next step

Reserve a few minutes for the changed world. Who trusts the party? What remains damaged? Which consequences still apply? What do the players want next? A concise record can make the next session easy to begin.

Ask one question about comprehension and one about enjoyment. “Which rule was hard to find?” produces a different answer from “Which choice did you want more of?” Treat those answers as evidence. The published book cannot substitute for observing your particular table.

Agree any BU rewards separately from the fictional gratitude or treasure. Do not award a mandatory amount just because the scene used combat. Let the table know how advancement will work in this campaign.

## A workshop in understandable rulings

The following cases are **optional example rulings**, not an extra mandatory tariff. Each shows how a DM can preserve creativity while identifying missing access, changed scope, and consequences. Use the actual circumstance at your table.

### The unseen target behind a wall

A player says, “I have Far, so I hit the guard in the next room.” Ask how the actor locates that guard and how the working is delivered. Ordinary hearing may reveal an approximate sound without fixing the exact location. A granted sense can supply more information if its rule actually does so.

A projected bolt is blocked by total cover on its path. Direct manifestation avoids intervening projected-cover penalties, but still needs valid targeting and the corresponding behavior. Far does not establish either. The player can target a known spot with an appropriate area construction and accept uncertainty, move to gain sight, use a relevant sense, or propose another authored targeting method.

Say which part is missing. “You know someone spoke in the room, but your present sense does not locate their body precisely; the wall also blocks this projected path” lets the player revise the attempt. “No, your spell cannot do that” does not explain the permission problem.

### A flood rather than a bowl

Mira's Water and Verb II support reshaping existing water. A bowl and a surging street are eligible subjects of different scale and pressure. Ask which portion she wants to change, where it goes, how soon, and whether the result must persist.

Offer alternatives tied to those facts: a narrow safe lane, help from workers with boards, or a slower diversion before the storm peak. An agreed cost might restrict Mira while she maintains the lane, damage an outlet, or create a later obligation. Do not convert every larger expression into fixed Vitality damage.

A larger cost does not give her Life access or guarantee rescue of every person in the water. If she wants to heal them as well, review that extra behavior explicitly. Maintaining the channel can spread pressure over time without a compulsory percentage discount.

### A small character and a large sword

The base equipped-slot rule is one for an ordinary item and two for a two-handed item. Size does not automatically multiply that final requirement. The Rule of Cool can support an unusual sword with its own authored slot cost or consequence.

Discuss how the character carries, braces, and uses it. A three- or four-slot requirement can make the choice meaningful. A restriction in cramped spaces may fit another concept. Record the actual item terms; do not create a secret size formula that changes unrelated equipment.

Keep Load, Item BU, ordinary value, and equipped use separate. A sword can be expensive in the setting without a large Item BU figure. Extra slots do not increase carrying capacity. Giving an unusual item permission to work in this scene does not grant automatic reach, damage dice, or resistance.

### A strong argument and a frightened person

An Influence check resolves uncertainty in an approach. It does not erase a motive or establish mind control. Ask what the speaker offers and what the listener reasonably believes.

A guard who fears punishment may accept a credible written authorization, a witness, or a plan to share responsibility. A large number alone is not the offer. If the response is secure once those facts are established, no roll is necessary. If uncertainty remains, name what success changes: a temporary passage, a private conversation, or willingness to hear evidence.

A capability that overrides agency belongs to a different construction and needs explicit behavior, recipients, resolution, limits, and consent at the table. Do not use ordinary negotiation as a covert substitute for that permission.

### A moment that matters more than a formula

A player wants to leap farther than ordinary movement allows to catch a falling companion. Establish location, available movement, actual reaction opportunity, and the physical route. The table may agree extra movement with a consequence or a bounded cinematic exception.

State the exception before resolution. Perhaps the leap reaches the companion but leaves both hanging from the ledge, or spends a resource and makes retreat difficult. If the proposed consequence is unacceptable, the player can choose another response. Spending a Reaction Slot alone grants neither unlimited distance nor guaranteed rescue.

Record the reason for the ruling: unstable ledge, visible falling ally, reachable handhold, agreed exertion. This preserves consistency without turning the exceptional rescue into permanent flight.

### A lasting injury without a hidden package

A character accepts “burned hands.” Ask what that does. A specific −2 to delicate Finesse checks is different from an inability to hold a tool, a reduction of Physical, or loss of an acquired permission.

Record recovery. Dressing and an agreed rest may clear a minor consequence; a severe injury can need another stated treatment. Healing Vitality does not automatically clear either unless the consequence says so. Avoid leaving a player with an undefined indefinite penalty.

Narrative description can coexist with a mechanical rule. The wrapped hands show what happened; the record explains how to play it. A later revision should be discussed, not silently broadened during an important action.

### An ambiguous new primitive

A player proposes “Always succeed at hiding.” Ask what information, recipients, conditions, and resolution the rule intends. It could mean a bonus, a result floor, invisibility, erasing evidence, or overriding others' perception. Those are different effects.

Help produce a narrower first version: a specific bonus to concealment through movement under stated conditions, or an authored sense-obscuring behavior with limits. Review price and stacking afterward. Do not assume a chosen BU number makes an unreadable rule understandable.

Test normal, stretched, and outside-scope uses. If a primitive's definition supports contradictory outcomes, fix the definition before judging its cost. A clear inexpensive rule is more useful than a grand title with a large number.

## Creatures from a purpose to a record

A creature begins with an encounter use, not a difficulty label. Ask what it wants, how it changes the scene, what players can perceive, and what can stop the confrontation without killing it. Then build the actual permissions supporting that behavior.

The records here are original **authored examples** using the current creature foundation and reference anchors. Example custom prices require table review. They are not new compulsory catalogue entries. Unspent budget is shown honestly; it grants no hidden ability.

:::art grazer

### The foundation arithmetic

Choose a positive base budget B. Rank is sqrt(B/25). Base attribute points are ceil(3 × rank); PB is 1 + ceil(rank); baseline maximum Vitality is max(1, ceil(B/2)). These are creature baselines, not the character's 10-point allocation and level-based Vitality formula.

Allocate nonnegative base attribute points and choose the proficient attribute. Read the full current parent attribute for each practice check, then its actual training and modifiers. Digital practice-allocation slices satisfy the allocation ledger; a small slice is not a second reduced check modifier.

| Chosen budget | Rank | Attribute points | Baseline PB | Baseline Vitality |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 25 | 1 | 3 | 2 | 13 |
| 50 | sqrt(2) | 5 | 3 | 25 |
| 100 | 2 | 6 | 3 | 50 |
| 225 | 3 | 9 | 4 | 113 |

At B 50, rank is not rounded to 1 before the other formulas. ceil(3 × sqrt(2)) is 5 and 1 + ceil(sqrt(2)) is 3. Round at the specified stage. If an explicit agreed Vitality override or active primitive changes the result, record it separately.

### Canal Watcher: a small guardian

Use the complete 25-BU Canal Watcher record in Worked records. Its 20 spent BU supports Verb II, Water I, Near, and d8. It has five remaining, no equipment, no mirror credit, and no unlisted action economy. Physical 2, Mental 0, Magical 1; Magical proficient; PB 2; DC 8; maximum Vitality 13.

Its value in a scene comes from turning a current near a spawning bed. Telegraph that concern before it attacks. Give it a path to negotiation: a credible diversion preserving the bed. Its location near useful water supports its capabilities; a dry bank changes its opportunities.

One Watcher can warn and manipulate a small channel. Two can guard different objectives, disagree about people, or cooperate. Do not treat quantity as an automatic license to apply the same consequence to every character. Each creature has its own intent, position, state, and reaction availability.

### Sluice Warden: a sturdier protector

**Purpose.** A constructed keeper braces damaged waterworks and prevents sudden releases. It is patient when shown a safe repair plan and forceful when someone tears a control away. Its visible tell is a rising arm pressed against the gate brace.

Base budget 100; rank 2; six base attribute points allocated Physical 3, Mental 1, Magical 2. Magical is proficient; baseline PB 3 and Vitality 50. One acquired Physical increment changes current Physical to 4. Magical-selected DC is 5 + 3 + 2 = 10.

Braced Resistance is an authored conditional defense with a proposed 8-BU price. It affects covered Physical damage, not every source, every consequence, or allies. Breaking the brace removes its qualification; merely naming it a Warden does not grant permanent armor.

**Derived checks and saves.** Physical practices +4; Mental practices +1; Magical practices +5. Saves Physical +7, Mental +1, Magical +5. Current Physical affects carrying capacity but does not change the base allocation ledger. Large size gives walking 40 ft, ordinary swim/climb 20 ft, six equipment slots, and capacity 80 + 5 × 4 = 100 Load.

**Guiding Wall.** Use Water, Verb II, Near, and Structure II to organize a located band of existing water into a temporary directed obstruction. State shape, affected route, opposition, and continuation. The normal example is a short lane beside the gate, maintained while braced. It does not automatically immobilize every creature or negate attacks; resolve actual crossings and interference.

**Pressure Strike.** Project existing water along a path within 30 ft. Attack +5 against the target's DC; normal hit expression 1d8 Magical Water. Cover applies to the projected path. Larger output or additional targets needs appraisal, not an automatic extra Main Intent.

**Equipment.** A Medium fixed-hook brace has Item BU 4, Load 2, and two final equipped slots. Its authored rule gives +2 to Prowess checks to hold a gate while the hook is firmly anchored; it affects that check, not Physical. Such qualifying Prowess is +6. Item BU stays separate from the 52 creature BU. Unequipping it removes the conditional equipment contribution and frees its slots, but its carried Load remains.

**Counter-approaches.** Show the brace and control route. A player can negotiate a safer flow, unanchor the hook, or draw the Warden away from the fixed sluice. A strong defense becomes an observable choice rather than a concealed statistic.

### Bellhouse Custodian: an asymmetric solo

**Purpose.** The Custodian guards a mechanism that records binding promises. It wants the bells in their proper order and will pursue someone who removes a marked counterweight. Its tell is a slow raising of two separate hammer arms: one protects the mechanism, one threatens the route.

Base budget 225; rank 3; nine base attribute points allocated Physical 4, Mental 2, Magical 3. Magical proficient, PB 4, Vitality 113. Magical-selected DC 12. Large walking 40 ft and swim/climb 20 ft; capacity 100 Load; six slots. Physical practices +4, Mental +2, Magical +7; saves Physical +4, Mental +2, Magical +7.

| Acquisition | Bound rule | Creature BU |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Verb II | Reshape/redirect eligible existing states | 8 |
| Metal I | Grounded metal subject only | 4 |
| Far | Reach up to 60 ft | 8 |
| d10 | Eligible output die | 8 |
| Structure II | Basic spatial/multiple-target arrangements | 8 |
| Plated Resistance | Halve Physical Metal damage, rounded up; no other source/domain defense | 8 |
| Second Response | One additional independent Reaction Slot per round, same ordinary trigger/scope limits | 12 |
| Total acquired | Explicit authored grants; no mirror credit | 56 |
| Remaining | 225 − 56 | 169 |

The conditional scope and 8/12-BU custom prices are example authoring choices. The large remaining budget is not converted into automatic damage, extra phases, or secret abilities. Its base foundation already differs greatly from a low-budget creature. You can develop the record further through real acquisitions when the campaign needs that, rather than spending numbers merely to fill a bar.

**Hammer Sweep.** Manipulate existing metal within 60 ft through an actual supported path. Normal single-target attack +7, 1d10 Magical Metal on a hit. A line affecting several recipients is a different declared expression supported by Structure II and appraised for scale, targeting, and consequences; the normal attack line does not silently contain it.

**Hold the Counterweight.** Redirect a located metal counterweight to hold the central lift. Continuing the example binds one hammer arm to that task. Releasing it changes the lift's position as agreed. It is a restrictive upkeep requirement, not a compulsory Vitality payment.

**Second Response.** Two slots are available at Council because the explicit acquisition says so. Each response still needs a perceived valid trigger, immediate eligible scope, and remaining movement or other actual access. A boss title, Large size, or 225 base BU would not provide these slots without the authored grant. The Custodian retains one coherent Main Intent by default.

**Practical solo design.** Threaten an objective as well as Vitality. The party may split between restoring bell order, keeping the lift open, and negotiating the promise's meaning. Tell them what the arms are doing. Let interference change opportunities. A second response makes the creature more responsive, not immune to every plan.

### Review before running

Read each creature's definition aloud in the voice of a new DM. Can you identify its attack bonus, damage source/domain, sense, path, reaction capacity, and ending conditions? If not, repair the record before it appears in a tense scene.

Test a favorable situation, a counter-approach, and a changed environment. The Watcher loses useful water; the Warden loses its brace; the Custodian must protect a lift while negotiating. Such tests reveal dependencies that a total BU comparison cannot.

Keep build and appraisal ledgers separate. Two Wardens are 200 enemy creature BU plus 8 Item BU, not 104 creature BU because each spends 52, and not 208 creature BU with equipment counted again. Quantities create independent creatures; they do not merge their Vitality into one shared bar unless a deliberately authored exception says so.

## Run an encounter through its whole life

Preparation, active play, and aftermath need different records. A compact preparation can become a useful run sheet without carrying every paragraph into every round. Keep definitions accessible, but track only the state that is changing now.

### Prepare the choices

Write the objective, opening situation, visible pressure, and plausible alternatives. Put distances, barriers, resources, and sensory conditions where you can find them. A roster alone is not an encounter: it does not say why people are present or what a good outcome might be.

Record Party BU and Party Item BU separately, with party size if known. Manual totals are sufficient. Linked character totals can help, but access to a sheet is not editing permission. In the digital workflow, calculated totals replace manual values only when the GM chooses to apply them; later changes should be refreshed intentionally.

Record each template's chosen base budget, exact version, Item BU, and quantity. A pinned definition makes the preparation reproducible. Updating a version can change the creature; review that change rather than assuming the same name means the same mechanics.

### Use comparisons as facts

Enemy count compared with party size tells you how many independent positions and responses may matter. A budget concentrated in one creature tells you that one loss or control effect can change the scene sharply. Several support creatures can create different pressures from a single opponent with the same total.

For a party of three default starting characters, Party BU is 75 before equipment. Two Watchers contribute 50 creature BU and zero Item BU. If party equipment is 8 Item BU, combined party is 83 and enemy is 50. Difference is −33; ratio is 50/83. This arithmetic says nothing automatic about safety. A flood, rescue duty, surprise treatment, or difficult targeting can dominate the actual scene.

Do not label this “easy” solely from the ratio. Describe control, area effects, support, burst, terrain, information, and alternative objectives. Comparable totals are not a promise of comparable difficulty.

### Start with independent state

Each creature needs its own current Vitality, position, consequences, reaction availability, and maintained work. Name repetitions clearly. Watcher 1 may negotiate while Watcher 2 remains angry; sharing a definition does not make their decisions or injuries identical.

In the platform, starting a run creates independent private play copies from the prepared pins. Starting another run creates fresh copies and preserves the earlier run. Previewing or adding a template does not create a creature immediately. Paper play can achieve the same separation with one short state line per creature.

### Council is a coordination point

Present the changed battlefield. Resolve agreed continuing requirements, reset ordinary independent reactions, and ask for coherent Main Intents. Record track placement and dependencies. Avoid carrying last round's resolved marker as a permanent status.

You can speak for several enemies without hiding their observable plans. “The Warden braces the gate while the Watcher turns toward the eastern channel” provides usable information. What the characters cannot perceive can remain uncertain, but all opposition should not become a private sequence of surprises.

### Resolve the changed scene

When intents intersect, decide whether the question is timing, directly opposed effort, hitting, or resisting. Use the matching procedure. Do not add a clash and a contest and an attack and a save to the same moment merely because all four exist.

A failed timing roll changes who acts first; it does not universally delete the loser's intent. A disrupted route can require a pivot. A reaction pauses its triggering event, resolves a valid response, then resumes from the new facts. Positions and movement used stay continuous across tracks.

Advancing the phase is an organizational action. It does not automatically damage creatures, expire consequences, heal characters, or resolve an upkeep requirement. Apply actual rules and endings deliberately. Digital state controls should support that work rather than invent it.

### End without erasing history

Mark the immediate result: objective achieved, retreat, negotiated agreement, changed control, or unresolved danger. Ending the encounter does not delete playable copies or erase consequences. A creature can remain injured; a promise can remain binding; a damaged place can matter next session.

Record who witnessed the result and which relationships changed. Ask players what they want to follow. Archive the run when useful. A future replay or new run starts its own state, not a retroactive rewrite of this encounter.

## Adventure: the gate that will not open

**Original teaching adventure.** A canal settlement faces rising water and a jammed sluice. Two guardians fear for a downstream spawning bed. Workers need safe access to the mechanism. This adventure teaches a visible problem, ordinary tools, supernatural access, negotiation, shared timing, and consequences that can be restrictive or environmental.

**Preparation.** Use two 25-BU Canal Watchers from Worked records, no equipment, 50 total enemy creature BU. This is a starting roster, not a difficulty guarantee. A party such as the three workshop characters has 75 Party BU; record its actual equipment separately. Reduce or omit opposition if your group's needs suggest it. The guardians do not attack simply because the record contains an attack.

:::map sluice

### Opening and immediate facts

Read or paraphrase: “Brown water climbs the gauge beside the sluice. Workers lean against a rope that shudders with each surge. Across the channel, a small river spirit points urgently toward reeds below the gate. Someone calls from a maintenance door: the upper wheel is jammed.”

Give visible distances from the map. The central gate is 30 ft from the western landing. The east bank is another 30 ft beyond it. A lower maintenance route runs 40 ft beneath the western wall. The spawning bed lies downstream, outside the immediate gate platform. The schematic labels measured routes; room footprints and line lengths are not to scale. There is no imported diagonal-movement rule.

The water is rising because a snapped timber blocks the wheel, not because someone casts a hidden spell. The guardians are protecting eggs and young river life. Workers want to prevent a release into the houses. All three concerns can coexist in a successful plan.

### People with understandable motives

**Ressa, repair worker.** Wants workers safe and the gate under control. Knows the passage and how the manual wheel should move. Offers tools and directions freely to people helping. Fears an uncontrolled release because her family lives downstream. She is not a quest dispenser waiting for an Influence roll to reveal obvious facts.

**Senn, boat runner.** Wants a boat unloaded before the quay floods. Has strong rope and a small flat-bottomed boat. Can help ferry people or haul material, but needs someone to secure the mooring. A fair plan can recruit that help without a supernatural compulsion.

**The two Watchers.** One points toward the bed; the other watches the gate. They can communicate through gesture and ordinary conversation at this table. Their Water capabilities do not grant mind reading. They believe people will open the gate without considering the downstream life. Showing a credible protected flow addresses their actual objection.

### Area A: the western landing

A solid lower post can anchor rope. Spare boards, a hook, and a marked maintenance arrow are available. Give these facts immediately when inspected. The post can support a rescue line; it is not infinitely strong. Agree demanding loads or sudden pressure with the actual circumstances.

The landing becomes less safe if the water continues to rise. Telegraph the change: water covers the lowest step, then the rope strains against its angle. Do not advance a hidden countdown every time a player asks a question. Use fictional time and the table's agreed urgency.

A player can recruit workers, examine the jam, or establish a line toward the wheel. Ordinary tools remain useful. A Near working from the landing can reach the gate at 30 ft if the actor can locate its eligible target; it cannot reach every point beyond it automatically.

### Area B: the wheel platform

A snapped beam is caught between wheel and brace. It is visible from a close safe angle. A Reason or Knowledge check can resolve diagnosis under pressure if uncertainty matters. If someone has secure access and enough time, simply explain the jam.

Removing the beam without controlling the release risks a sudden flow. Establish that before commitment. Torren might hold the wheel with tools; Mira might redirect a narrow portion of water; Ilex might coordinate workers. Their chosen sequence affects complexity and track placement. The DM does not require one fixed “repair capability.”

A Watcher can move toward the platform to prevent an uncontrolled opening. Telegraph its intent. If timing matters, resolve the actual clash. If two actors struggle over the wheel, resolve the contest. Use the question that exists, rather than forcing both procedures onto every interaction.

### Area C: the maintenance passage

The lower passage is wet, narrow, and partly blocked by debris. An arrow and Ressa's information reveal it. A printed map on the workshop wall provides a third source. No single failed search prevents discovering every alternative.

The route reaches the underside of the wheel. It can support a careful removal while someone controls the upper mechanism. Water level and tools matter. The party may prefer this route because it avoids direct confrontation on the platform; it introduces a different exposure and rescue problem.

If someone slips, locate them and explain the actual danger. A nearby catch can use an eligible reaction. A remote rescue requires relevant access and reach. Stabilization, healing, and escape remain separate questions if a character collapses.

### Area D: the spawning bed

The reeds visibly shelter small river life. A Watcher points to them before using violence. Characters who investigate learn why the guardians resist. A credible protected flow can turn opposition into cooperation.

The bed is not a mandatory sacrifice. Players can use boards, a temporary diversion, a slower gate opening, or another plan. An extraordinary attempt must still have its eligible subject and behavior. Water shaping supports a flow plan; it does not automatically heal the eggs, rewrite the guardians' agency, or repair every structure.

### Pressure and possible escalation

Choose changes from the visible situation. The lower step floods. The wheel tilts. A mooring loosens. A worker calls that a storehouse is taking water. Give warning and a usable response before a route disappears when the fiction allows it.

Do not attach a fixed number of rounds to the whole adventure unless your table intentionally agrees a timed treatment. Negotiation might resolve before a combat round begins. A secure repair can take minutes. A sudden slip can require the shared rhythm immediately.

If the group refuses the problem, portray a plausible aftermath. Workers evacuate, the guardians protect what they can, and the canal route may close. Their later adventures begin from that result. The world changes without punishing players simply for choosing another goal.

### Three plausible resolutions

**Cooperative diversion.** The party explains a safe downstream route, recruits workers, and controls the release. The Watchers help instead of attacking. Costs can be sustained attention, damaged boards, or a repair obligation. Celebrate the actual plan rather than requiring a fight for completion.

**Forced opening.** The party drives off the Watchers and removes the beam. If they do not protect downstream life or houses, apply those established consequences. Defeating the guardians does not automatically solve the hydraulic problem. Their independent states and choices continue until the threat ends or they retreat.

**Evacuation and delayed repair.** The party secures people and accepts temporary closure. The immediate threat to life ends, but boats and supplies cannot pass. The group may need materials or specialist help next session. This is a different success, not a failed version of your preferred solution.

### Aftermath and rewards

Record saved people, damaged places, guardians' attitudes, equipment use, current Vitality, and remaining consequences. Agree recovery from the actual shelter and time available. A night spent repairing in the rain is not automatically a full long rest.

If this completes a significant campaign objective, discuss advancement according to your campaign agreement. No universal BU award is attached to two defeated Watchers. Ressa's gratitude, a new route, and a promise of passage are fictional results whether or not the ledger changes.

Possible next questions: Who installed the brittle beam? Why has the upstream flow changed? What old channel became visible? Use a question the players cared about. The adventure has taught how to respond to a situation, not how to follow one mandatory plot.

## Adventure: the bellhouse of borrowed names

**Original teaching adventure.** A hilltop bellhouse stores public promises in metal counterweights. An old agreement now appears to bind the wrong families. The Custodian refuses to release the central lift while its records conflict. The party must establish what changed, protect people using the lift, and choose what to do with the agreement.

This is a situation about evidence, obligation, and an asymmetric guardian. It can end through restoration, negotiation, changed terms, or force. The adventure does not presume the promise is just because an ancient mechanism preserves it.

**Preparation.** Use the 225-BU Bellhouse Custodian from Creatures from a purpose to a record. Optional two Canal Watchers, reflavored only in story as rain-fed caretakers, add 50 creature BU without changing their Water mechanics. Total with both is 275, no Item BU. This can be a severe threat to low-budget characters. Compare your actual party, telegraph danger, and offer meaningful noncombat routes; do not claim the example is balanced by its page count.

:::map bellhouse

### Opening and what is wrong

Read or paraphrase: “A bell sounds once, then stops halfway through its answering note. The lift hangs above the lower landing. On the hill, bronze arms brace two different counterweights. A keeper holds a cracked metal plate: the names stamped on it do not match the charter.”

The record was not maliciously altered by a hidden mastermind. Years of repair replaced one counterweight with an old spare whose binding marks belonged to another agreement. A hurried keeper failed to check the replacement. You can choose a different cause for your campaign, but establish it before clues depend on it.

The central question is not “win the boss fight.” It is what the party will do with conflicting evidence and a mechanism that acts on the marked metal. People below need a safe route; the keeper needs an understandable resolution; the Custodian needs a credible account of the bells' order.

### Area E: the lower landing

The landing gives sight of the lift, 30 ft above. A stair ascends 50 ft along the outer wall to a gallery. A service path leads around the hill, longer but sheltered. The schematic identifies these alternatives and labels measured route distances. Read those labels instead of measuring the drawing.

Ordinary ladders, ropes, and workers can support a rescue. Flight or mobility grants change the route only within their actual rules. A single character reaching the lift does not automatically transport everyone. Locate passengers and explain who needs help before resolving a rescue.

The lift remains held while the Custodian maintains Hold the Counterweight. A loss of that requirement changes its state at the agreed instant. Give characters visible information about the braced arm and actual supports; do not hide a falling-lift consequence behind an innocent control button.

### Area F: the archive room

There are three useful sources: the paper charter, a maintenance ledger, and the keeper's account. The charter lists the original parties. The ledger records the replacement. The keeper saw the old spare taken from storage. Each offers a route toward the mistake.

Inspection can reveal obvious differences. A Knowledge or Reason check can resolve reading a damaged entry under pressure. Ilex's Paper access can stabilize the physical sheet at Touch; it does not create missing words or prove truth automatically. Ordinary careful copying can also preserve the record.

Optional discoveries include an earlier dispute, an unfulfilled repair promise, or evidence that the original agreement unfairly burdened one group. These add decisions. They are not necessary to discover the mechanical replacement error.

### Area G: the bell gallery

Three bells face different paths. Their visible marks correspond to the charter's three witness places. A worker can explain the ordinary order. The Custodian compares actual ringing and metal marks; it does not read every person's thoughts.

Players can show the mismatch, restore the old counterweight, propose a temporary suspension with an accepted obligation, or ask whose authority can change the agreement. Decide the Custodian's response from its purpose and the evidence. An Influence check may resolve uncertainty about a credible proposal, but it does not rewrite the mechanism's metal by itself.

If someone removes a counterweight without support, the Custodian responds. Telegraph which arm moves and whether the lift is threatened. Its two Reaction Slots are explicitly authored; they are not an automatic privilege of every solo creature. It has one coherent Main Intent by default.

### Area H: the counterweight chamber

The chamber contains the substituted weight, the old damaged original, and tools. Existing Metal I and Verb II can support reshaping or redirecting eligible metal, but deleting a social obligation or changing a person's identity is not simply more metal manipulation.

A physical repair can restore the original mechanism while leaving the moral question unresolved. A table might agree a bounded exceptional working to transfer a mark with consequences; state its actual scope. Do not secretly grant foundational law rewriting because a character rolls high against a mechanical task.

The chamber is 40 ft from the gallery through an open service corridor. A closed maintenance door can block a projected path. Valid direct manifestation still requires a target location and supported behavior. Map lines and walls have practical meaning, not merely decorative shapes.

### A confrontation with several useful jobs

If the party chooses force or the scene escalates, give them several purposes: hold the lift, keep the archive safe, distract the Custodian, or restore the bell sequence. Killing it is not the only useful Main Intent. Different positions and dependencies keep the shared round coherent.

The Custodian's normal Hammer Sweep is one target, attack +7, 1d10 Magical Metal on a hit. A larger declared expression requires the actual access and appraisal described in its record. Its Plated Resistance covers Physical Metal damage only. Other eligible sources and domains are not secretly resisted. Announce visible deflections so players can understand their options.

Hold the Counterweight commits an arm. A player who makes that maintenance unnecessary may change the creature's choices; one who disrupts it may endanger the lift. Show these trade-offs before resolution. A solo threat is more interesting when the scene offers decisions than when it simply attacks until its Vitality reaches zero.

### Resolutions and what they leave behind

**Restore the original record.** The replacement mistake is corrected. The lift operates. The original agreement remains, including any unfair terms the party discovered. People may thank them and still ask for further change.

**Negotiate a temporary release.** The keeper and relevant people accept clear terms: who will witness the correction, when the bells will be checked, and what protection applies meanwhile. Record the obligation. The Custodian can accept it if the established purpose supports that response; an unexplained good roll is not a universal override.

**Replace the agreement openly.** The party helps the affected people choose new terms. This can require time, witnesses, ordinary records, and a reviewed extraordinary working if the mechanism needs one. Do not assume destroying a counterweight is identical to lawful agreement, or that ancient machinery determines the table's moral answer.

**Destroy or abandon the mechanism.** The immediate guardian threat may end, but the lift, records, and community face new problems. Apply the visible consequences. Give the players a world that can respond without declaring every unconventional choice a failure.

### Debrief the adventure

Ask which evidence mattered and which assumptions the characters changed. Check rescue outcomes, equipment, Vitality, obligations, and any maintained working left active. Agree rests through the actual shelter and circumstances.

For future sessions, choose a thread the players pursued: repaired trade, disputed authority, a promised witness, or an unexplored service route. Record new acquisitions only through the campaign's agreed advancement. The adventure demonstrates flexible preparation with a complete creature record, not a compulsory setting for all SwordWeave tables.

## Build a campaign that can respond

A campaign needs continuity more than a complete encyclopedia. Keep a small record of places, people, pressures, and promises that have already mattered. Let player choices create the next preparation.

### Make factions useful at the table

For each group, write a desire, a resource, a constraint, and a visible next step. Canal workers want safe routes and possess tools, but cannot replace a major gate overnight. Guardians want protected habitats and can change water, but disagree about human cooperation. A records office has documents and authority, but depends on people accepting its legitimacy.

These facts let you respond to negotiations, delays, and discoveries. They do not need a compulsory numerical influence score. Add a campaign tracker only if it helps your group, and label it as an optional tool.

Avoid deciding that one faction is secretly correct about everything. Give people partial information and understandable conflicts. Players can learn, choose allies, and revise their own assumptions.

### Let time change something visible

Between sessions, ask what each active pressure does if nobody changes it. A damaged gate stays closed. A keeper seeks a witness. A guardian moves a spawning bed. Show signs of those developments when the characters return.

Do not make every pause cause disaster. Safe downtime can be valuable. The pace of change should follow the world and the agreed campaign tone. A recurring clock is optional guidance, not a core mandate to punish rests.

### Award growth transparently

Discuss whether progress follows major objectives, regular review, smaller awards, or another agreed rhythm. No compulsory XP schedule applies. Explain when an award changes cumulative progression and which purchases it supports.

Keep ordinary treasure value, Item BU, Load, and acquisition BU distinct. A valuable heirloom can be mechanically ordinary. A useful item can have low setting value. An equipment reward needs its actual rule and availability, not just an impressive price tag.

Review proposed new ingredients with the player. If a reward would force their character into a role they do not want, offer alternatives. Growth should support the next choices rather than turn the construction game into a fixed class ladder.

### Repair a rule without rewriting trust

When an authored rule causes problems, name the observable issue. It may be unclear targeting, duplicated acquisition references, unbounded duration, or an automatic action the author did not intend. Discuss revisions between scenes when possible.

Keep a brief change note and the version used in play. Agree replacements and state adjustments. Changing a maximum does not automatically heal current Vitality; changing access does not silently erase consequences. Do not use a rules correction to invalidate an earlier choice made under the old agreement.

### Preserve useful session records

At the end, record current goals, important positions, ongoing requirements, consequences and their endings, rescue/recovery state, and acquired changes. A concise record beats a transcript that nobody can search.

Shared sheets have actual permissions. A DM preference shows controls but does not grant private access. A character being linked to an encounter does not authorize the GM to edit it. Decide who can make changes and preserve independent creature state across runs.

### Prepare what you will actually use

Before the next session, choose a likely opening, two or three pressures, and a few adaptable resources. Reuse a creature definition when it fits, but change its motives or location openly. If you change its mechanics, record a new definition rather than relying on an old name.

Keep one unanswered question for each player if useful. Those questions can guide scenes without determining their answers. A campaign remains a shared story because the preparation creates choices and the players' responses change it.
